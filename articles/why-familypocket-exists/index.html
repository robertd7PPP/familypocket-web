Why FamilyPocket Exists: Pocket Money Before Kid Banking

Why I built a pocket money app with shared balances, easy kid access and a clean design, for the stage before kid bank accounts.

By FamilyPocket 31 October 2026 4 min read

We were using a whiteboard to keep track of the kids’ pocket money. Before building FamilyPocket, I tried some other apps. They were okay, but they didn’t bring together the things we needed in the way I wanted: access across devices, job approvals, a clear history and a view the kids could use themselves.

We also didn’t want to open bank accounts for the kids at that stage. We wanted to keep track of their pocket money while we still handled the real money.

So I built a prototype. The kids understood it straight away and started using it. Seeing that response was what led me to build a version for the app stores.

FamilyPocket grew out of that practical problem in our own family. I wanted the everyday parts of pocket money to work together, with a fresh, simple design that both parents and kids could understand.

If you’re choosing an app, our pocket money app comparison puts FamilyPocket alongside other named options, with features, free limits and payment models side by side.

The everyday parts needed to work together

A balance is one part of the picture. We also wanted to see what had changed, let the kids look at their own information, and have a way for them to claim jobs for a parent to approve. Those things needed to work across the family’s devices.

In FamilyPocket, a child can claim a completed job on their linked device. A parent approves it, and the amount is added to the child’s recorded balance. The history gives the family a record of the changes. Balances, jobs and wishlists are there for parents and kids to see within the same household.

The family still decides how much allowance to give and which jobs to pay for. I’ve written separately about our approach to allowance and how we distinguish ordinary chores from extra paid jobs.

A clean screen that makes sense

I wanted FamilyPocket to feel fresh and simple, with clean aesthetics and an uncluttered interface. Parents and kids should be able to understand what they’re looking at and start using it easily.

Adding a kid’s device should be easy

I wanted adding a kid’s device to be as easy as possible, without requiring a separate login for the child.

FamilyPocket uses a household join code to link a device. Kids don’t need their own email address or a separate personal sign-up. Once linked, they can see their balance, transactions, wishlists and jobs, and claim jobs for a parent to review.

The app records balances agreed within the family. The real money stays outside it: FamilyPocket doesn’t hold funds, transfer money or issue a spending card. That is the stage it serves—keeping pocket money clear while parents still handle the real payments.

Value matters too

I also wanted a low subscription price. For the things our family needed, I didn’t feel the apps I tried offered the right value for the amount being charged.

Manual deposits and spending entries in FamilyPocket are free and unlimited. The first 20 job approvals or direct job payments are also included, shared across the whole household. That is a one-off household total. Once those are used, Family Plan is needed for further job payments and automatic allowance. Manual tracking remains free.

When comparing costs, it helps to look at the features your family will actually use, what remains available free and what starts a paid subscription.

Why compare it with other apps?

There are other ways to manage pocket money, and families will want different things from an app.

Card services such as Greenlight in the US, GoHenry in the UK, Spriggy in Australia and SquareOne in New Zealand include a way for children to spend real money. That may be useful when a child is ready to make purchases independently.

There are also other trackers, including Chores & Allowance Bot and Bomad. FamilyPocket is one option among them. The combination of features, design, access and cost is what a family needs to weigh up.

We put together the comparison using public product pages, help documents, store listings and review input. It groups tracking apps, hybrid tracker/card services and banking/card apps, so you can first consider what kind of system you need. The notes explain plan and country differences, and Unclear marks details we couldn’t establish.

My reasons for building FamilyPocket came from what we wanted at home. The comparison gives you a way to consider it against what your own family needs.
