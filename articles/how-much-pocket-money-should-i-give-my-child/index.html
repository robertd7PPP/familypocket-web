How Much Pocket Money Should I Give My Child?

We put $15 a week into each child's allowance and investments. Here's how savings goals, jobs, and a six-to-eight-week horizon shape that number.

By FamilyPocket 13 August 2026 6 min read

We put $15 a week into each of our two kids' allowance and investments.

That sounds simple, but there are lots of details we've considered behind that number. Two parents can both say "$15 a week" while meaning completely different things.

We originally said to the kids, "We'll give you $10 a week. If you choose to put any of it into long-term investing, we'll match that amount. The split is your choice." They both chose a 50/50 split of that $10: $5 became their regular allowance, $5 went into a long-term investment, and we matched that $5 into the same investment. That makes $15 going into the system each week: $5 for current spending and $10 invested.

The investments are made into a Total World ETF.

What do your kids want to buy with their allowance?

We try to set things up so a meaningful spending goal is around six to eight weeks away. That's long enough to practise waiting, but close enough to keep the motivation alive.

An $80 Lego set is a current example. The honest maths is that $5 a week only adds up to $30 after six weeks or $40 after eight. That leaves $40 to $50 to find, so the regular allowance won't buy the set by itself.

Small additions for completing weekly jobs help. For a bigger goal like this, negotiated extra jobs add a lot to the balance. Reaching it in six to eight weeks therefore depends on those extra contributions or money already saved. The allowance keeps the goal moving every week, while extra effort gives them a way to bring it much closer.

The set also gives us something real to talk about. What's its usual price? How many weeks of saving will it take? Would an extra job be worth doing? If the price falls from $80 to $60, is it a genuine sale?

We've also watched them learn this from experience. They once bought something after we'd told them it was often on sale. They wanted it then and went ahead anyway. When they later saw it at the sale price, they were disappointed. Now they pay much more attention to the usual price of future purchases, how often it is discounted and when another sale is likely.

Having money already saved means they can take advantage of a good price when a sale appears. They can also decide the set isn't worth weeks of saving and work after all. That's a useful decision too.

They chose the split

We didn't decide that half of the $10 had to be invested. When we introduced long-term investing at around age 12, we told the kids we'd match whatever part of that $10 they chose to put aside. They chose $5.

That choice is important. They could have kept more as their regular allowance, but putting $5 into the investment turns it into $10 through our match. They can't use that money until they're 18 or older.

Now we can talk about two time frames. One part might become Lego in a month or two. The other is being left alone for years. The kids can see what regular contributions, investment growth and time might do, while knowing that long-term money isn't available for the next exciting purchase.

How jobs fit in

Our standard weekly expectations include folding their own washing, helping with the dishwasher, taking out the rubbish, and cleaning the bathroom. They're part of carrying the household load.

We add small amounts - usually around 10-30 cents - when those jobs are completed. The amount partly reflects how often the task needs doing. These small additions encourage consistency and help the routine stick.

Jobs beyond the normal expectations are negotiated separately. These are age-appropriate jobs that genuinely help the household. Mowing the lawns can save us from paying someone else to do it. Gardening can save us a considerable amount of time.

Because the work has real value, it makes sense to agree on fair payment. That opens another set of conversations: What's a fair price? How long will the job take? How much closer will it move the savings goal?

The kids have a regular $5 amount they can count on, small encouragement for keeping up good habits, and a way to earn more when a bigger goal needs it.

Give the allowance a bigger job as they grow

Right now, we still cover meals out, family activities, and similar costs. Their regular $5 allowance is for their own spending choices.

As they get older and start going out without us, we'll change that. Some of the money we'd previously have spent directly on food, movies, or other outings will become part of their allowance.

We won't fund unlimited outings. They'll have to choose. A movie might mean skipping food out, or they might skip both and keep saving.

That's when an increase makes sense to us: when the allowance has a bigger job.

A common New Zealand rule of thumb is $1 per year of age each week. It gives parents a useful number to react to, but age can't tell you what the money covers or what choices you want it to create. Our own $15 is a total going into the system, not a $15 spendable allowance: $5 is available for current spending, while $10 goes to long-term investment. Small job payments can add more when we agree to them.

Keep the conversation going

We don't sit the kids down for a formal money lesson. The conversations come from whatever is happening that week:

  • What are you saving for?
  • What's the normal price?
  • Is the sale price actually good?
  • How many weeks away is the goal?
  • Would an extra job be worth it?
  • How much do you think that extra job is worth?
  • If you buy this now, what else will have to wait?
  • How much do you want to keep for later?
  • How are your investments doing?

That everyday conversation is worth more than finding a supposedly perfect rate. OECD's PISA 2022 results found that 64% of participating 15-year-olds discussed their own spending decisions with parents weekly or monthly, and those students tended to perform better in financial literacy. It doesn't prove that talking causes the difference, but it supports making money a normal family subject.

So how much should you give? Pick a real savings goal. Choose a time frame that feels motivating. Decide how much progress should come from the regular allowance and whether extra jobs can bring a bigger goal closer. Then check that the amount fits your family budget and what the child is expected to cover.

For us, the answer is $15 a week. But there is a lot behind that number.
