Allowance and chores

Should Kids Get Paid for Chores?

In our household, regular allowance covers normal responsibilities, tiny additions recognise consistency, and bigger jobs are negotiated separately.

By FamilyPocket 4 September 2026 6 min read

Should kids get paid for chores? In our family that all depends on what type of work it is.

Our kids share in the everyday chores that keep the household running. If that work is always left to one or two people (the parents!), the burden quickly becomes unfair.

Our regular allowance covers those normal household expectations as a whole. We add a small amount when a chore is completed - usually around 10-30 cents - to encourage consistency and help the habit stick. If the job is missed, they miss that small addition but their regular allowance does not change.

Work that sits outside the normal routine and provides real additional value to the household is negotiated separately.

Some jobs are part of living together

Folding their own washing, helping with the dishwasher, taking out the rubbish, and cleaning the bathroom are part of the work created by living together. Everyone uses the dishes. Everyone creates washing and rubbish. Everyone benefits when the bathroom is clean.

We do not put a separate price on each of those contributions. Ten cents is obviously not the market value of emptying the dishwasher. It is simply a small prompt that helps keep the routine going.

When the kids were younger, an ordinary request could be accompanied by plenty of moaning. Over time, the jobs became part of the family routine and expectation. Now they are generally done without the resistance that used to come with being asked.

The change happened gradually, and we would not expect every child to respond in the same way. What matters in our household is that the expectation has become normal. The money is not the reason for the chore.

Bigger jobs are a separate conversation

Some work saves us a significant amount of time or replaces something we might otherwise pay someone else to do. That means it has a different kind of value to us.

For those jobs, we negotiate a separate amount. The conversation is not only about whether the kids want more money. It includes what needs to be done, how long it might take, how difficult it is, and what the work is worth to the household.

We do not want every contribution to family life to become a negotiation. We also do not want to pretend a substantial extra job is the same as taking a share of the everyday work.

Getting the price wrong was part of learning

When the kids were younger, they would sometimes negotiate way too low.

They would agree on an amount, start the job, and then realise it was taking much longer than they expected. Part-way through, they would come back and negotiate upwards.

This was ok as they were still learning how to judge the size of a job, estimate their time, and decide what the effort was worth. We didn't just try and catch them out with a bad first estimate. We wanted to help them negotiate better next time.

Slowly, their judgment improved. Experience gave them something real to base the next conversation on. They became better at thinking through the job before agreeing on the amount, rather than discovering the problem only after the work had begun.

The lawns gave them a starting point

When they were old enough to mow the lawns, the kids asked what we used to pay the person who had done the job for us.

That was a sensible starting point. The previous price showed that the work had genuine value to the household and gave them a real number to consider.

It was not automatically the amount we would pay them. We were providing the tools and setup and we needed (at least at the start) to help them through it safely. They understood that difference and accepted a lower amount, but it was still meaningful enough to reflect the work involved.

By then, the negotiation had become more considered. They had an outside reference price, thought about what was and was not included, and reached an amount that made sense in our household.

Questions that help define an extra job

There is no universal price list that will work for every family. Before agreeing on a paid job, it can help to talk through a few practical questions:

  • What does “finished” look like?
  • How long does the child think it will take?
  • Is it part of their normal household contribution, or is it genuinely extra?
  • Does it save the household a meaningful amount of time or replace something the family might otherwise pay for?
  • Who is providing the tools and materials?
  • Is the job safe and appropriate for that child?
  • If an inexperienced child has badly misjudged the work, can the estimate be revisited?

There is no proven payment system

Families draw these lines differently. Some give allowance with no connection to household jobs. Some make the full allowance conditional on a set of chores. Others pay task by task.

New Zealand financial educator Dr Pushpa Wood takes a similar view, describing common household chores as part of being in a family. Her broader point is that there is no single right answer: parents need a rationale they can explain, and they should leave room to revisit it as children grow.

Research gives us reasons to include children in age-appropriate household work, but it does not settle the payment question. One large US study found an association between doing chores in early primary school and later self-competence, prosocial behaviour, and self-efficacy. An Australian study found an association between some household chores and working memory and inhibition. Neither study showed that chores caused those outcomes, and neither compared paid chores with unpaid ones.

Research on rewards is not a simple answer either. One major review found negative effects in several reward conditions, while another argued that those effects were more limited. The result depended on the task, the person's existing interest, and how the reward was offered. Those experiments were not tests of real family chore systems.

For us, that uncertainty is another reason to use a practical household rule rather than claim there is one proven system.

Where FamilyPocket fits

The family still has to decide what is expected and what a bigger job is worth. FamilyPocket does not make that judgment for us.

Once we have agreed that a job will be paid separately, we can put the job and its amount into FamilyPocket. The kids can claim it when it is done, we approve it, and the amount is added to the balance we track for them.

That keeps the agreement and the result visible while leaving the judgment with the family.

So should kids get paid for chores? In our household, it depends on whether we are asking them to take their share of ordinary family life or offering a separate piece of valuable work. Helping everyone understand the difference is what we are trying to teach our kids.
